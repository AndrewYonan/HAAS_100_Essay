The Big Short
Andrew Yonan — November 6, 2026

This age will see a shortage of hard, honest work. The uprootings of AI have touched the classrooms, the office jobs, the sciences—even the intractable, sublime art of mathematics—and now, regretfully, storytelling—one of the oldest and most endearing forms of art. We can pass our calendars, our tax returns, our cover letters on to the machines, but what happens when the machines come for our hobbies too? Wasn't art, however hard, supposed to be fun?

In the wake of a suspicious Wall Street Journal op-ed, its author, investor Stanley Druckenmiller, publicly admitted to pulling the work off an AI. Druckenmiller's reluctance to tag the work as AI-generated drew a slew of calls for disclosure, to which he replied, matter-of-factly, "Disclose what?" He went so far as to say "I write everything using AI now for the same reason I use a calculator when I do math problems" (Oremus). Druckenmiller's shamelessness is commendable, and he can be forgiven for invoking the calculator analogy, at least in part because he is an investor and not a writer. Although I've chosen to use his words here because they mirror a broader trend in our endorsement of AI writing—a crippling obsession with productivity. Of course, non-creative technical work has a defensible claim to AI integration—writing in these domains is purely functional, so it naturally demands (and is reasonably entitled to) optimization. And it's no surprise that a billionaire investor would recruit AI to write his op-ed, for the same reason that a star college athlete might recruit a friend to do their math homework for them. However, the op-ed is a reasonably creative piece of writing—it lends the author the very intimate right to crack their shell of formality, to pass glimpses of their soul. To trade such an opportunity for productive convenience is a blemish of taste, if not a notch in our humanity. Perhaps alarmingly, Druckenmiller's sentiments aren't limited to the STEM sphere; case studies indicate creative writers themselves are adopting AI-integrated routines in favor of productivity, despite vicious public outcry against AI in the arts. Writers professional and amateur are turning to AI in droves, dispatching the hairiest and gnarliest of their qualms to their favorite chatbots, and recruiting machines as instant editors. This frenzy not only refuses to admit the emptiness of AI-generated prose, but is attempting to fix a problem that was just as much a godsend as a headache—the pain of writing.
