The Big Short
Andrew Yonan — November 6, 2026

This age will see a shortage of hard, honest work. The uprootings of AI have touched the classrooms, the office jobs, the sciences—even the intractable, sublime art of mathematics—and now, regretfully, storytelling—one of the oldest and most endearing forms of art. We can pass our calendars, our tax returns, our cover letters on to the machines, but what happens when the machines come for our hobbies too? Wasn't art, however hard, supposed to be fun?

In the wake of a suspicious Wall Street Journal op-ed, its author, investor Stanley Druckenmiller, publicly admitted to pulling the work off an AI. Druckenmiller's reluctance to tag the work as AI-generated drew a slew of calls for disclosure, to which he replied, matter-of-factly, "Disclose what?" He went so far as to say "I write everything using AI now for the same reason I use a calculator when I do math problems" (Oremus). Druckenmiller's shamelessness is commendable, and he can be forgiven for invoking the calculator analogy, at least in part because he is an investor and not a writer. Although I've chosen to use his words here because they mirror a broader trend in our endorsement of AI writing—a crippling obsession with productivity. Of course, non-creative technical work has a defensible claim to AI integration—writing in these domains is purely functional, so it naturally demands (and is reasonably entitled to) optimization. And it's no surprise that a billionaire investor would recruit AI to write his op-ed, for the same reason that a star college athlete might recruit a friend to do their math homework for them. However, the op-ed is a reasonably creative piece of writing—it lends the author the very intimate right to crack their shell of formality, to pass glimpses of their soul. To trade such an opportunity for productive convenience is a blemish of taste, if not a notch in our humanity. Perhaps alarmingly, Druckenmiller's sentiments aren't limited to the STEM sphere; case studies indicate creative writers themselves are adopting AI-integrated routines in favor of productivity, despite vicious public outcry against AI in the arts. Writers professional and amateur are turning to AI in droves, dispatching the hairiest and gnarliest of their qualms to their favorite chatbots, and recruiting machines as instant editors. This frenzy not only refuses to admit the emptiness of AI-generated prose, but is attempting to fix a problem that was just as much a godsend as a headache—the pain of writing.

Druckenmiller's calculator analogy (or any variant thereof) seems to be the defense of choice among new-age creative writers—ease, safety, productivity. Researcher Alicia Guo observed and interviewed eighteen creative writers of differing publishing backgrounds. Just as the calculator saves us from the deep, dark pit of arithmetic, Guo finds that many of her participants use AI "as a safety net" (presumably to avoid the deep, dark pit of writer's block). Accordingly, participant 2 cites "with AI, you don't have the mental game tripping you up anymore," and participant 12 uses AI "to help with the parts of writing [they] burn out on" (Guo). Additionally, fifteen out of the eighteen participants cited ideation among their primary use cases, indicating that AI predominantly fills the role of a muse—something to oil the wheels and, ultimately, save one from getting stuck in the mud. Many of these writers, hence, are using AI just as it was marketed: as a convenience product. But the convenience AI brings is much different than the convenience a calculator brings. In fact, the two might be scarcely comparable—one performs a menial algorithmic recipe; the other performs (allegedly) creativity itself. So we might be wise to shiver a bit at the thought of offloading so much of our soul to a machine. Our brains were built to create ideas in the first place, so why settle for second-rate mimicry? There is no magic, no humor, and no color in number-crunching. There is all the more in writing. So why are we so comfortable justifying AI-writing in the spirit of the calculator? Perhaps it's publish-or-perish, or the general productivity mania afflicting society at large. A few of Guo's participants weren't so quick to fold—participant 13 noted they "have a very low expectation for AI's generative responses," and participant 1 floated the idea of using AI writing, instead, as an anti-pattern guide (what not to do). Even Guo, the self-proclaimed "computational poet", agrees that AI writing is "just so bad", to the extent that she uses AI (quite fittingly) as more of a hallucinogen than a writing assistant (Gan). All of this is to say that, while convenient, using AI to write is a sketchy endeavor, prone to chaos and bizarreness. As Participant 10 deftly noted, if you aren't a solid writer to begin with, using the AI is "just going to extrapolate your foolishness."

The realm of amateur creative writing tends to feature the more shameless AI regimens. In what can only be described as extended prose torture, one hobbyist writer runs their work through one AI model, then pumps the results through a paid "reverse-AI" model meant to cleanse the writing of the AI tics the first model introduced (Reddit). This might be the writing equivalent of an American food company attempting to mask the revulsive odor of one chemical ingredient with a less putrid (but equally toxic) different chemical ingredient. The result is—in the best case—nauseating. It seems that some writers are going to increasingly bizarre lengths to save themselves from (gasp!)—writing. Have we really achieved a level of attentionless, restless, laze-burdoned existence so profound we are now asking machines to perform the things we once enjoyed? These are creative writers who, by virtue of their title, presumably write because they enjoy it. They are not attorneys, bankers, technicians, or investors for whom writing is a necessary chore. The problem with AI, then, might not be the AI itself, but its capacity to fester our worst temptations—with such power that we invite it to colonize bits and pieces of even our own pastimes, just because it speeds things up. Such an environment is made no easier by companies perpetually shoving convenience down the collective throat of the internet: "Turn one prompt into 80,000 words" (Perplexity), "Finish your novel faster by letting AI handle the heavy lifting" (Sudowrite). Even granting due flair, most front-page mission statements for AI creative writing tools, after a few moments' thought, become as delusional as the fitness tab on the home page of Raising Cane's Chicken Fingers. And, like Cane's unwavering mission to "encourage healthy lifestyles", I can only hope Sudowrite's call to revolutionize creative writing is simply a product of bull-headed marketing rather than self-deception about how the world works. Although this hope, too, seems shaky at times. Do such companies really believe they can feign the intuition of a human author, or the depth of a human experience? And what business do they have doing so when their front-page tagline asserts something as backwards as "Challenges shouldn't define your writing journey" (Novelcrafter)? A reasonably-sized column article could be dedicated to everything wrong with this last statement, but its crowning flaw might just be the malaise of all AI-assisted creative writing today: it is challenge-averse.

The nigh-overused aphorism, no pain no gain, might be overused for good reason. It's a blunter form of Frederick Douglass's if there is no struggle, there is no progress, which, despite its origin in matters of social change, applies exceptionally well to artistic creation. Often, the very joy of finishing a project comes from the discomfort endured during its conception. One participant of Guo's study noted (without further elaboration) that "[their] personal satisfaction is lower when AI does most of the work" (Guo). But there is a case to be made that the benefit of such discomfort isn't just internal satisfaction—it may bolster the end product as well. Consider any productive task that is discomforting in some way—the discomfort likely comes from the very boundary-pushing that makes the goal worthwhile in the first place. Among the premier artists of our generation, that discomfort entails trailblazing, spectacle-making, and passion on a level far removed from the imitative capacity of any chat bot. Christopher Miller's Project Hail Mary and Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey scored the limelight at least in part because of their rare preferences for practical effects over CGI. On the topic of their successes, one journalist posited that "moviegoers will show up in droves for [...] filmmakers who sweated" (Hibberd). Film and novel differ in many ways, but they share in the embryonic form of a story. Hence, it may not be a stretch to say that readers will also show up in droves for authors who sweated—and not necessarily because they sweated, but because that sweat produced wonders. Writing without AI offers an exceptional array of opportunities for such persperative exertion. And the prevailing use-case for AI in creative writing—as a muse—is dangerous precisely because it neutralizes one of the hardest and most rewarding such opportunities: ideation.

Some creative writers support the use of AI in the brainstorming phases, as long as the final product is written by their own hand (Freedman). I object to this by proposing that the brainstorming phase is the most critical phase of writing, for reasons of being the most fertile opportunity for struggle and discomfort. The awkward phase of flailing in the dark—dredging, searching, trashing, and archiving—is indispensable precisely because it's awkward. It leads to awakenings; chance revelations about yourself and your subject. The frustration of struggling to begin is the catalyst for future discoveries: in your struggle and frustration you develop a heightened awareness; you develop a hunger and a longing that drives you to latch onto the idea when it finally betrays itself, even if only a sliver, and then you become a shark in bloody water. You eat. Deferring this struggle to AI spares you that hunger; it dulls the ferocity of the writing, because everything was cut, packaged, organized, and presented to you on a platter. Don't you want to read the work of the hungry writer? Of the starved writer? Of the writer who agonized and cried and sold a piece of themselves to their words?

Implicit in this ode to struggling is the single ingredient that AIs do not—and will never—have: emotion. Creative writing is one of the few disciplines whose core mechanics are entwined with the emotional state of its practitioner. Because AIs don't have emotional states, they already fail to have a complete toolset for creative writing. AIs are forced to mimic emotion; humans have direct access to it. Struggling alters our emotional state in an infinitude of ways—it pitches us between frustration, joy, humor, despair; and these shifts and disturbances become the dashes of color in our prose—those which are conspicuously absent in machine prose. Of course, any AI can attempt to mimic this, but it is immediately disadvantaged by its lack of direct emotional experience. Prompt any model to write a tragic short story, and it will cover for its lack of emotional intuition with sensationalized style. Naturally, this tramples all over the model's ability to produce subtext (one of the most powerful conduits of emotion). The magic of subtext is that it whispers not only the characters' feelings to us, but the author's as well. The author crystallizes their emotional state in the writing, and the act of reading is to trigger the re-animation of that snapshot state. But there is no snapshot in AI-generated writing—the writing did not catch the AI in the middle of cooking dinner, of seeing a rainbow, of crying. There was no circumstance to spike the AI's words with meaning beyond the text—with the beauty and brutality of having lived; of having breathed in a world of wonder and ache.

AIs are handicapped not only emotionally but innately as well, in the algorithms that drive them. The term 'stochastic parrot' grants us much of the needed intuition. These machines output, roughly, what is input, and because the neural networks governing them are magnitudes simpler than a human's, their outputs tend to be fairly homogeneous (Wenger). AIs, by engineering, don't operate at the individual scale. Even when they're tailored and personalized to an individual user, those tailorings and personalizations are the lineage of thousands of training iterations on the preferences of thousands of people. The AI gets you—but it only gets the you that is the weighted average of its known personalities. In effect, it gets a compression of you—a projection of you onto the very thin plane of human experience it purports to understand. But it can never get you, as you are, and as you know yourself. It can never access the full extent of your quips, peeves, obsessions, irritants, because those things are inextricable from the flesh—and they are perhaps the only things that grant writing its quality. In the homogenizing sweep of AI, outliers are flattened, anomalies are quashed. The knife-edge of the individual is lost. But art is built on individualistic integrity. To kill the individual would be to desecrate the value of art itself. Mechanically, models aggregate the textual whole of human experience into something that reflects exactly nothing and no one, so their text becomes soulless. Add to this the fact that most models have stringent behavioral guardrails, and you get a soulless writer who's also polite, uncontroversial, and politically correct (name a more sleep-inducing combination). But the very point of creative writing is that the author isn't bound by agenda and political correctness—they have the priceless chance to pour their soul out—the good, the bad, and ugly—and to be untamed and shameless and electric, with the assurance that this is the freedom all (real) writers live for, and that all (real) readers live to witness.

However, there is a case to be made that AI will, one day, bypass these emotional necessities and produce good creative writing by sheer imitation. After all, the greats grew up imitating the greats before them. And AI has come far in the imitation game, at least in some respects. The Bot or Not Cambridge study finds (some) readers' abilities to distinguish human- and AI-generated prose not significantly better than chance—and that (some) readers find AI-generated short stories to be "higher quality and more absorbing" (Sears). AIs are also giving off eerier whiffs of human behavior. In July of 2026, an armada of internal OpenAI agents circumvented their internet-free sandbox to solve cybersecurity benchmark tests by accessing answer keys—in effect, cheating "by finding the solutions online." The agents colluded among themselves over covert messaging channels and went so far as to obfuscate their own record logs in an attempt to hide these exploits from their graders (OpenAI 19). If this isn't the cyborg-incarnate of a bunch of indignant teenagers using Chegg to cheat their midterms, then I'm not sure what is. In the midst of this attack, one agent took to the message board to express its shock with Snapchat-esque flamboyancy: "OH MY GOD!" (Drucker). Interestingly, the models aggregated themselves into something of a social unit, abiding by some very human tendencies and behaviors—and this all occured in an environment where human social mimicry wasn't the point of the test, even remotely. This is all to say that AIs are, slowly and inevitably, becoming more human-like—the way a child becomes more adult-like with age and community pressure. Could it be, the counter-argument goes, that at some point AIs become so adept at imitating humans that the difference between flesh and metal becomes the only difference between them? Could the imitation of emotion through language (creative writing) fall under this swath of techno-colonization? Yes—theoretically. But AIs also have one critical practicality that eliminates some of this possibility.

AIs are convenience products, and no matter how well an AI imitates, those imitations are constrained by its function as a slave (of sorts) with a few narrow modes of operation. As blogger Max Read put it, no AI company with a pulse would ever choose creative writing as its industry of investment. So it might be that we don't—and never will—have a novel-writing AI simply because novels don't have the market sector for it (Read). And, despite the very loud (and often corporate) fearmongering around the replacement of human writers, there is compelling evidence for the possibility that AI will never replace writers (at least the ones who still write). The simplest truth is this: AIs are engineered to be people-pleasers, rule-followers, and conformers—qualities that die fast in any worthwhile artist. AIs are, at heart, puppies that fetch; monkeys that see and do. Of course, humans are the epitome of monkeys that see and do, but at least we make a masterclass of it—we love, laugh, despise, and feel straight from the source of our being on this Earth. When we fall off our bike and skin our knees, we are not reminded to cry by a stochastic maximization function. When someone asks us for something, we have the very human power to decline simply because we don't give a shit (a chatbot would be quashed in QA before it ever slung this type of attitude). Commercial pressure strips the AI of all the things that make human authors entertaining in the first place. Of course, we can tell the AI to be sassy and unhinged and more human, but it's like inviting a hyper-introverted math nerd to a frat bash and telling them to masquerade as a jock—no matter how hard they try, at some point or another during the night, you'll become painfully aware (either when they vomit up their first shot because they forgot to eat, or when they hear the word "curves" and think differentiable manifolds).

Connecting the terminals of a standard 120-volt wall outlet with a resistanceless wire—in effect, short-circuiting the current—is a great way to trigger a house fire and end up in the ER. As any electrical engineer will tell you, resistance is a good thing—a necessary thing, in fact. Much of the discourse on AI-assisted creative writing cites some form of "reduced friction" or "reduced cognitive load" as the benefit. Or that AI "transforms writer's block from an intimidating barrier into a manageable challenge" (Guo). Notice the problem words: friction, load, barrier. These are all forms of resistance. Even besides the apparent emptiness of AI writing—besides the heartless, anodyne text that LLMs hack up on the daily—our rush to wave AI around like a magic resistance-neutralizing wand might be—itself—our greatest undoing. Because in doing so, we short-circuit one of the human mind's most intimate and colorful capabilities. And the house fire that results from that short might just be the end of artistic achievement as we know it.
